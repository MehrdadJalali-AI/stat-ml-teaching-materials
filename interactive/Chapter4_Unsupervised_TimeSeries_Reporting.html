Section 1

Cluster analysis: discovering natural groups

Clustering groups similar data points without predefined labels. A good clustering has high similarity within each cluster and clear differences between clusters. It also has to be interpretable: you should be able to describe each group and act on it.

🏥 Healthcare

Group similar scans, tissues or patient profiles

📣 Marketing

Segment customers by behaviour or preferences

🔎 Search & recommendation

Group similar pages, products or users

📄 Documents

Organise reports, articles or emails by topic

🌐 Web analytics

Find visitor behaviour patterns

🚨 Anomaly detection

Points that belong to no cluster are suspicious

Quality depends on choices you makeThe result depends on the distance or similarity measure, the algorithm and its parameters (K, eps…), and the feature scaling. There is usually no single “correct” clustering.
Section 2

K-Means: assign, update, repeat

1Choose K
→
2Initialise K centroids
→
3Assign each point to the nearest centroid
→
4Update each centroid = mean of its points
→
5Repeat until nothing changes
Objective: minimise SSE (inertia) = Σclusters Σpoints ‖x − centroid‖²
Run K-Means by hand

✓ Advantages

Simple, fast, scales to large data, always converges, works well for well-separated, roughly round clusters.

✗ Disadvantages

You must choose K. Sensitive to the initial centroids (a different start can give a different answer; use several runs / k-means++). Struggles with outliers, noise and non-spherical shapes.

Section 3

How many clusters? Evaluating clustering

Compactness: SSE / inertia

Always decreases as K grows. Look for the elbow, where adding another cluster stops helping much.

Separation: silhouette

s = b − amax(a, b)

a = mean distance to the point's own cluster, b = mean distance to the nearest other cluster. Range −1…+1; higher is better. Other options: Davies–Bouldin (lower is better), Calinski–Harabasz (higher is better).

Elbow method and silhouette score (same data as above)

Also check stability (do repeated runs agree?) and interpretability (can you name each cluster, e.g. “young high-spenders”?).

Section 5

DBSCAN: density-based clustering

DBSCAN defines clusters as dense regions. A core point has at least min_samples neighbours within distance eps. Border points are within eps of a core point but are not core themselves. Everything else is noise. It needs no K, finds irregular shapes, and flags outliers.

Section 6

PCA: seeing high-dimensional data in 2-D

Real data has many features (age, income, purchase amount, frequency…). Principal Component Analysis builds new axes. PC1 is the direction of largest variance, PC2 the next largest at a right angle to it, and so on. Plotting PC1 vs. PC2 gives a 2-D picture of the data, which is mainly how PCA is used in a clustering workflow: to visualise and explain clusters.

Find PC1 yourself: rotate the projection axis
Reading a PCA plot of clustersEach point is coloured by its cluster label. Well-separated colours mean distinct groups. If two clusters strongly overlap, they may not be truly different, or the first two components don't capture what separates them. Check the explained variance and try other components.

Section 7

What makes time series different, and decomposition

Order matters

Observations come at t = 1, 2, 3, …, and the timeline is part of the data.

Past influences future

Previous values help predict the next ones (autocorrelation).

Patterns over time

Trend, seasonality, noise and sudden shocks.

Evaluation is different

Never shuffle: train on the past, test on the future. Forecasts are one-step or multi-step ahead.

Additive: y = Trend + Seasonality + Residual  ·  Multiplicative: y = Trend × Seasonality × Residual
Decompose AirPassengers (monthly, 1949–1960)

Trend

Long-term direction: more smartphone users each year; web traffic slowly falling after a campaign ends; stable electricity demand.

Seasonality

A pattern repeating at fixed intervals (day, week, month, year): Christmas and Black Friday sales peaks, summer air travel.

Residual / noise

What is left after removing trend and season. Ideally random around 0; a huge single residual is an anomaly, such as a market crash or an outage.

from statsmodels.tsa.seasonal import seasonal_decompose
res = seasonal_decompose(df["passengers"], model="multiplicative", period=12)
res.plot()        # observed, trend, seasonal, resid
Section 8

Stationarity and differencing

A stationary series has a stable mean, a stable variance, no trend and no changing seasonal pattern. Classical models such as ARMA/ARIMA assume it. The usual fix is differencing (yt − yt−1) to remove trend, seasonal differencing (yt − yt−12) to remove seasonality, and a log to stabilise growing variance.

Section 9

Time-based split and baseline forecasts

Never shuffle a time seriesA random split puts future months into the training set. The model then “predicts” the past using knowledge of the future (leakage), and its scores are far too optimistic. Instead, train on the first ~80 % of the timeline and test on the last ~20 %. For CV, use TimeSeriesSplit (expanding window).

A baseline is the simplest sensible forecast. Every advanced model must beat it. If it can't, the extra complexity is not worth it.

Naive

ŷ = last observed value

Seasonal naive

ŷ = value from the same season last year (this December ≈ last December)

Moving average

ŷ = mean of the last w values

Forecast the last 24 months of AirPassengers

MAPE = mean(|y − ŷ| / y) × 100 %. It is scale-free and easy to explain, but undefined when y = 0. Holt-Winters is a classical exponential-smoothing model with level, trend and season, fitted here on log(y).

Section 10

The forecasting model landscape

Yt = c + φ₁Yt−1 + φ₂Yt−2 + … + φpYt−p + εt

Autoregressive: predicts from past values of the series itself, each with a weight φ. p = how many lags. Good for short-term dependence in stationary data.

Yt = c + εt + θ₁εt−1 + … + θqεt−q

Moving-average model (not the same as a moving-average smoother!): predicts from past forecast errors (shocks). If the model just made a big error, it adjusts the next prediction. q = how many past errors.

ARMA(p, q) = AR + MA for stationary data. ARIMA(p, d, q) adds the Integrated part: difference the series d times to remove the trend. SARIMA(p, d, q)(P, D, Q)s adds seasonal AR, differencing and MA terms with season length s (s = 12 for monthly data with yearly seasonality). Typical uses: retail, energy, tourism, weather.

from statsmodels.tsa.statespace.sarimax import SARIMAX
m = SARIMAX(train, order=(1, 1, 1), seasonal_order=(1, 1, 1, 12)).fit()
fc = m.forecast(steps=24)
Forecast = Trend + Seasonality + Holidays + Error

Prophet (Meta) is built for business series with weekly/yearly seasonality, holidays and events, missing values and outliers. It is easy to use and its components are interpretable, but it is less flexible than fully custom models. Best for sales, web traffic and demand.

XGBoost and Random Forest do not understand time order by themselves. You turn the series into a supervised table of lag values, rolling means, calendar features and external variables (weather, promotions). They handle non-linearity and many features, but need feature engineering, and you still need a time-based split.

Build lag features

The first 12 rows have empty lag_12 and must be dropped. Every feature may only use information available before time t.

LSTM

A recurrent network with memory gates that captures long-term dependencies. Needs lots of data and compute.

GRU

A simplified LSTM: fewer parameters and faster, with similar results on many tasks.

Transformer

Self-attention processes the sequence in parallel; strong for long, complex series. Very data- and compute-hungry.

Hybrid models (ARIMA-LSTM, ETS-ANN) combine a linear model for the structure with a neural network for non-linear leftovers.
