Section 2

Types of data: categorical vs. numerical

Categorical · qualitative

Describes categories, not quantities. Examples: gender, blood type (A, B, AB, O), department, colour.

Can be nominal (no order: blood type) or ordinal (ordered: low / medium / high). Summarise with counts and the mode.

Numerical · quantitative

Represents measured or counted values. Examples: age in years, income in €, temperature.

Can be discrete (countable: number of customers) or continuous (measurable: height). Summarise with mean / median and SD.

Sort these variables
Trap: numbers that are really categoriesPostal codes, student IDs and phone numbers are stored as numbers, but the mean of a postal code means nothing. Ask yourself: does arithmetic on this column make sense?
Section 3

Measures of central tendency: mean, median, mode

Central tendency gives the single most representative value of a dataset, which tells you where its centre is.

Mean

x̄ = 1n Σ xi

Blood pressure 120, 130, 110, 140, 125 → 625 / 5 = 125 mmHg. Uses every value, so a single outlier can pull it a long way.

Median

middle value of the sorted data

Ages 22, 28, 35, 40, 50 → 35. With an even count (…, 35, 40, …) take the average of the two middle values: (35+40)/2 = 37.5.

Mode

most frequent value

Blood types O, A, O, B, O, A → O. This is the only centre measure that works for categorical data, e.g. the most chosen streaming platform.

Mean vs. median with an outlier

Edit the numbers (comma-separated) or use a preset. The dot plot marks each measure.

Why the median matters in MLSensor readings 22, 23, 98, 22, 23: the mean is 37.6 (misleading) while the median is 23 (the real signal). Robust statistics like the median make features and imputation less sensitive to noise spikes.
Section 4

Measures of dispersion: variance, standard deviation, range

Two datasets can have the same mean and still look completely different. Dispersion measures how far values spread around the centre, which tells you how consistent and reliable the data is.

Variance σ²

σ² = 1n Σ (xi − μ)²

Average squared distance from the mean. Its unit is squared (cm², €²), which makes it hard to read directly. Used inside ANOVA and regression.

Standard deviation σ

σ = √σ²

Same unit as the data (cm, €), so it is the one you report and explain. Roughly the typical distance of a value from the mean.

Range

max(x) − min(x)

Heart rates 60…100 bpm → range 40. Quick but rough: depends only on the two most extreme values and is very sensitive to outliers.

Same mean, different spread

Start from Slido's dataset B = [5, 10, 15, 10, 10] (mean 10). Set the multiplier to 0 to get dataset A = [10, 10, 10, 10, 10]. All values are equal, so every deviation is 0 and the variance and SD are both 0.

Why dispersion matters in MLFeature selection: a feature with near-zero variance carries almost no information. Scaling: features with very large variance need standardisation. Uncertainty: widely spread errors mean low confidence and may signal overfitting.

Addition rule (A or B)

P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B)
Build events on a die

Click a face once to add it to A, twice for B, three times for both, four times to clear. Slide example: A = even, B = greater than 4.

Multiplication rule (A and B)

Independent events

P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B)

Two coin tosses: ½ × ½ = ¼. The first toss doesn't change the second.

Dependent events

P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B | A)

Bag with 3 red, 2 blue, no replacement: 3/5 × 2/4 = 3/10. The first draw changes what is left.

Simulate: drawing two balls
Class exercise: box of 10 chocolates (4 dark, 3 milk, 3 white). Try it first, then reveal.
  1. P(dark or milk) = 4/10 + 3/10 = 0.7 (addition rule, no overlap)
  2. P(both white, no replacement) = 3/10 × 2/9 = 6/90 ≈ 0.067 (dependent)
  3. P(two students both get milk, with replacement) = 3/10 × 3/10 = 0.09 (independent)

Clinic version: 30 % diabetes + 20 % hypertension, no overlap → 0.5. A test that is 80 % accurate, used on two infected patients → 0.8 × 0.8 = 0.64.

Section 7

Probability distributions

A distribution describes how likely each value is. Discrete distributions cover countable values (customers, defects, pass/fail). Continuous distributions cover measurable values (height, time, temperature).

discrete Number of successes in n fixed trials, each with two outcomes and the same success probability p. Examples: defective bulbs in a batch of 10; how many of 200 survey respondents like a product (p = 0.6).

discrete Number of events in a fixed interval when events happen independently at an average rate λ. Examples: calls per minute at a call centre; accidents per month at an intersection; rare events.

continuous Symmetric bell curve defined by its mean μ and standard deviation σ. Mean = median = mode. Examples: adult heights, measurement errors.

continuous Every value in [a, b] is equally likely. Examples: random number generation, a fair lottery draw.

Section 8

Skewness: how lopsided is the distribution?

Skewness measures how asymmetric a distribution is around its mean. Watch where the mean, median and mode end up.

Skew it

Positive (right) skew

Long tail to the right; mode < median < mean. Income, house prices, waiting times. In finance, high-risk, high-reward investments.

Symmetric (skew ≈ 0)

Mean ≈ median ≈ mode. The normal distribution. If all exam scores are identical there is no asymmetry, so skewness is 0 (strictly it is undefined, because SD = 0).

Negative (left) skew

Long tail to the left; mean < median < mode. Scores on an easy exam, or age at retirement.

Why normal-looking data is easier to modelMany methods (t-tests, linear-regression inference, z-scores) assume roughly normal data or errors. Strong skew pulls the mean and inflates the variance. Common fixes: a log transform, using the median, or robust models.
Section 9

Sampling: learning about a population from a subset

A population is everyone you care about (all students in the programme). A sample is the part you actually observe (60 students). Sampling is cheaper and faster, and in practice usually the only option. It only works if the sample is representative.

Four sampling methods

100 students in 4 faculties (colours) and 10 classrooms (rows). Pick a method and draw a sample of about 20.

Stratified vs. cluster: the classic confusionStratified samples some members from every subgroup, e.g. randomly from each faculty in proportion to its size. Cluster takes all members of some groups, e.g. pick 5 schools at random and survey everyone in them.
Section 10

The Central Limit Theorem (CLT)

Take many random samples of size n and compute each sample's mean. With a large enough n, the distribution of those sample means is approximately normal, even when the population is not. Its centre is μ and its spread (the standard error) is σ/√n.

Store sales simulation

Population (sales of all stores)

Sampling distribution of the mean (0 samples)

Why it mattersThe CLT is why normal-based tools such as confidence intervals and t-tests work for averages of non-normal data. A common rule of thumb is n ≥ 30. Notice that the spread shrinks like 1/√n, so quadrupling n halves the standard error.
Section 11

Estimation: point estimates and confidence intervals

Point estimate

One best guess: “average height of the 30 students in our sample = 170 cm”.

Interval estimate (CI)

A range of plausible values: 170 ± 2 → [168, 172] cm. A narrower interval means more precision; a wider one means more uncertainty.

CI = x̄ ± tcrit · s√n
What does “95 % confidence” actually mean?

The true population mean is μ = 125 mmHg (dashed line). Each horizontal bar is the CI from a separate sample. In the long run about 95 % of the intervals contain μ. Red intervals missed it.

InterpretationA CI is not a guarantee, and it is not “95 % probability that μ is in this particular interval”. It reflects the long-run reliability of the procedure. Real examples: hospital blood pressure 122–128 mmHg; light-bulb lifetime 980–1,020 h.
Section 12

Hypothesis testing and reading a p-value correctly

H₀ · null hypothesis

No effect, no difference, no relationship. “The two study methods give the same average score.”

H₁ · alternative hypothesis

There is an effect, difference or relationship. “The averages differ.”

The p-value answers one question: if H₀ were true, how unusual would data at least this extreme be? We never prove H₁; we check whether the data are inconsistent with H₀.

See the p-value as an area
Say this ✓“The result is statistically significant at the 5 % level.”
“We found evidence against the null hypothesis.”
“We did not find enough evidence of a difference.”
Not this ✗“p = 0.03, so H₀ has a 3 % chance of being true.”
“p > 0.05, so H₀ is true.”
“We proved the treatment works.” · “Small p = big, important effect.” · “Significant, so X causes Y.”
Section 13

Testing differences and relationships

All three ask the same kind of question: is the pattern big compared with the natural random variation?

Compare the means of two groups. New drug vs. standard drug (blood sugar), version A vs. B of a web page (click-through rate), machine 1 vs. 2 (defects).

Try it: keep the difference fixed and raise n, or lower the SD. The same 5-point difference goes from “not significant” to “highly significant”. That is why statistical significance is not the same as practical importance.

Compare three or more means at once using one F-test, e.g. traditional vs. online vs. blended teaching, or three ad campaigns. Running many t-tests instead would inflate the false-positive rate.

F = variance between group meansvariance within groups

A significant ANOVA tells you at least one group differs, not which one. Follow it with post-hoc tests such as Tukey HSD.

Pearson r measures the strength of a linear relationship, from −1 to +1. Examples: study hours vs. exam score, exercise vs. cholesterol, time on site vs. purchase amount. Click the plot to add points and watch r change; one outlier can create or destroy a correlation.

Correlation ≠ causation, and r only sees straight linesIce-cream sales and drowning are correlated because summer drives both. In the U-shape example r ≈ 0, yet y depends on x completely.
import numpy as np, pandas as pd
from scipy.stats import pearsonr
np.corrcoef(hours, score)[0, 1]      # arrays -> r only
r, p = pearsonr(hours, score)       # r + p-value
df.corr(numeric_only=True)          # full correlation matrix

t, p = stats.ttest_ind(a, b, equal_var=False)              # 2 groups (Welch)
